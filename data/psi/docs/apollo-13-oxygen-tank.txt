## Mission
Apollo 13 was planned as NASA's third crewed lunar landing, targeting the Fra Mauro region.
Prime crew: Jim Lovell (Commander), Ken Mattingly (Command Module Pilot), Fred Haise (Lunar
Module Pilot); backups Jack Swigert and Charles Duke. The Saturn V was mated with the spacecraft
on December 10 and moved to Launch Pad 39A five days later for testing.

## Launch and Pre-Launch Problems
Charles Duke exposed the crew to German measles, so Swigert replaced Mattingly, who lacked
immunity. Five and a half minutes after liftoff, one S-II (second stage) engine shut down early;
the remaining engines burned longer to reach orbit.

## Accident
On the third day of the mission, after a 49-minute TV broadcast, oxygen tank number two exploded
and damaged oxygen tank number one. Jack Swigert's report to Mission Control is remembered as
"Houston, we've had a problem." Two of the three fuel cells were lost; the Service Module vented
oxygen into space, threatening loss of all electrical power and water. The Command Module was
declared "mortally wounded." The lunar landing was cancelled and the crew moved into the Lunar
Module, Aquarius, which had its own oxygen supply and descent engine, using it as a lifeboat.

## Recovery
Teams in Houston improvised solutions, including adapting the Command Module's square
carbon-dioxide (lithium hydroxide) canisters to the round openings in the Lunar Module's
scrubber. Water was rationed, power was limited, and the cabin became very cold. The crew shed
the Service Module before splashdown in the Pacific Ocean on April 17, ending the six-day
mission.

## Cause (combination of mistakes + deficient design)
- In 1965 the Command Module was modified to raise permissible heater voltage to the oxygen tank
  from 28 to 65 volts DC.
- The thermostatic switches on the heaters were NOT modified to match the higher voltage.
- During a final launch-pad test the heaters stayed on for an extended period, reaching about
  1,000 degF (538 degC), degrading the Teflon wire insulation.
- The tank was overheated for ~8 hours, leaving it vulnerable the next time it held oxygen.

## Investigation (Apollo 13 Review Board)
Chaired by Edgar M. Cortright; final report released June 15, 1970. The board found the accident
resulted from a combination of mistakes and a deficient design, with the root cause the
overheated oxygen-tank heaters that degraded their insulation during the pre-launch test.

## Lessons Learned / Design Changes (Apollo 14 and later)
- Redesign and recertify the Service Module oxygen storage system; NASA-wide review of all Apollo
  systems using cryogenic oxygen.
- A third oxygen tank added in a different bay; thermostats upgraded; stirring fans removed.
- All electrical wiring sheathed in stainless steel. (Apollo 1 lessons had already increased
  wiring insulation, which helped prevent arcing during the reentry power-up.)
- Strengthened management/engineering oversight of subsystems.

## Why it matters for FLAMEPRINT
Apollo 13 shows the hazard of high-energy oxygen systems and thermal/electrical faults (Teflon
insulation degradation) — relevant to PTFE wire insulation in the material list and to the value
of redundant oxygen systems, echoed later by the Mir fire.
