## History
After completing the Gemini program in 1966, NASA turned to the first crewed Apollo mission,
designated AS-204 and later named Apollo 1. It was planned as an open-ended Earth-orbit flight
of up to 14 days, targeting launch in early 1967, to prove the spacecraft was spaceworthy and
to test the Service Module's large engine. Command Module 012 was shipped to Kennedy Space
Center in August 1966 and moved to Launch Pad 34 in January 1967. During a late-January
pre-launch test, a fire killed the crew. The investigation found technical and management
failures; NASA responded with a redesigned spacecraft, a new hatch, and tighter controls on
combustible materials.

## Mission
The flight was scheduled for February 21, 1967 as a 14-day Earth-orbit test of a Block I
Command and Service Module (CSM). Objectives: test the spacecraft's life support systems and
the main Service Propulsion System (SPS) engine, with eight SPS test firings planned (the final
firing intended to deorbit the spacecraft for reentry on its 208th revolution). North American
Aviation (Downey, California) built the Apollo spacecraft under a 1961 contract, in two crewed
versions: the basic Block I (Earth-orbit testing) and a Block II with docking capability for
lunar missions. The crew trained extensively in Apollo CM simulators at the Manned Spacecraft
Center (now Johnson Space Center) and Kennedy Space Center. A full-duration flight would have
lasted 330 hours 50 minutes, breaking Gemini VII's endurance record.

## Crew
Virgil "Gus" Grissom (Commander) and Edward White (Senior Pilot) were spaceflight veterans;
Roger Chaffee (Pilot) was a rookie. NASA announced the crew on March 21, 1966.

## Accident
On January 27, 1967, during a preflight "plugs-out" test on the launch pad, with the crew in
spacesuits, hatches closed, and the cabin pressurized with 100% oxygen, a fire broke out and
killed all three astronauts. The pure-oxygen atmosphere caused the fire to spread extremely
fast, with a rapid rise in temperature and pressure that ruptured the Command Module pressure
vessel. The fire spread so fast there was no time to open the hatches. Autopsies concluded the
astronauts died of asphyxia from carbon monoxide and other toxic gases; their burns were
likely survivable.

## Investigation (Apollo 204 Review Board)
Established January 28, 1967 by Deputy Administrator Robert C. Seamans; it set up 21 panels and
released a 3,000-page report on April 9, 1967. It could not identify a single ignition source
but found evidence of arcing in several wires near the environmental control system (the area
of most severe damage). The three-piece hatch took ~90 seconds to open under ideal conditions
and could not be opened under increased pressure. The unfueled-rocket test had been deemed
non-hazardous and was not reviewed by safety organizations, and no defined crew-rescue process
existed.

## Lessons Learned / Engineering Changes
- Later crews flew the Block II Command Module.
- The three-piece hatch was replaced with a unified hatch that opens from inside or outside in
  ~3 seconds.
- NASA severely restricted and controlled combustible materials in the Command Module and the
  Lunar Module; new spacesuits used fire-resistant materials.
- Management and safety-culture changes followed congressional hearings.

## Why it matters for FLAMEPRINT
Apollo 1 is the canonical demonstration that atmosphere composition (pure O2) and
flammable-material selection dominate spacecraft fire risk — exactly the prevention axis
FLAMEPRINT scores. It motivates the oxygen-threshold and material-verdict logic.
